Estonia's Education System for Kids: From Public Kindergartens to International Schools

August 2026 · 14 min read

If you're relocating on your own, school for kids probably isn't on your radar. But if you're moving with family, it's one of the first things worth thinking about, ideally before you even leave. Queues for public kindergartens in Tallinn can get long, and international schools usually have limited spots per academic year. In this post I want to break down how Estonia's education system for children works, from public options to international ones, so you can start researching early.

The Overall Structure

Estonia splits children's education into a few stages. Kindergarten, or lasteaed, covers roughly ages 1.5 to 7. It's not mandatory, but every municipality is legally required to provide a place if a parent applies. After that comes basic school, or põhikool, for ages 7 to 16, which is compulsory by law. Then there's gümnaasium, general secondary education for grades 10-12, which isn't mandatory but is still free if you attend a public school.

All public education in Estonia is free, including textbooks and school lunches at the basic school level. What parents typically pay for is kindergarten (subsidized by the municipality), extracurricular activities, or private and international school options.

Public Kindergarten (Lasteaed)

If you're registered as a resident of a municipality (Tallinn, for example, once you've sorted out an official address as covered in the relocation paperwork post), you're entitled to apply for a spot at a public kindergarten in that municipality. Registration usually goes through the municipality's online queue system. For Tallinn, registration and queue status are handled through the self-service portal at info.haridus.ee/lasteaiakoht, and general information about the Tallinn preschool system is on the official Tallinn Education Department page.

A few things worth knowing about the public kindergarten queue:

  • The queue is based on your registered home address, so the closest kindergarten is usually prioritized, but a spot there isn't guaranteed.
  • Monthly fees are relatively low since they're subsidized by the municipality, plus a small daily meal fee calculated by actual attendance.
  • Most public kindergartens use Estonian as the language of instruction. Some cities, including Tallinn, also have Russian-language kindergarten options.
  • If your child is younger than kindergarten age, or you haven't secured a spot yet, private daycare or childminder options can be found through a few online platforms that let you filter by the caregiver's language and location.

My advice: apply as soon as your official address is registered, since queues in popular areas like the city center or newer business districts can get fairly long.

Basic School (Põhikool)

Basic school in Estonia runs for 9 years and is split into three stages, each with a somewhat different curriculum approach:

  • Stage I (grades 1-3): focused on basic literacy, reading, math, and adjusting to the school environment.
  • Stage II (grades 4-6): subjects become more varied, taught by subject-specific teachers rather than a single homeroom teacher.
  • Stage III (grades 7-9): preparation for the basic school final exams and the transition to gymnasium or vocational school.

Placement at public schools is usually based on your home address catchment area, similar to a zoning system. You can check the list and map of schools in Tallinn through the official Tallinn Education Department page or the national school directory at teatmik.haridus.ee/schools. Registration usually opens a few months before the new academic year starts in September, so if you're moving mid-year, it's best to contact the Education Department of your city directly for off-cycle enrollment.

For kids who don't yet speak Estonian, this is often a top concern for parents who just relocated. Some public schools in Tallinn offer extra language support or Russian-language instruction, but fully English-medium public school options are still very limited. This is one of the main reasons a lot of expat families end up choosing international schools, at least for the first year or two while their child adjusts.

General Secondary Education (Gümnaasium)

Gümnaasium covers grades 10-12 and isn't compulsory, but it's still free at public schools. Admission is based on basic school final exam results, and sometimes an additional entrance test depending on the school, especially for higher-reputation gymnasiums or ones with a specific focus like sciences or languages.

For official information on the overall Estonian education system, including the national curriculum and current policy, check the Estonian Ministry of Education and Research (hm.ee).

Private and International Options

If budget allows, or if your child needs a smoother transition without speaking any Estonian yet, private and international kindergartens and schools are a popular choice among expat families in Tallinn. Curricula are typically in English, following international standards like IB or Cambridge, with a fairly diverse student population by nationality. A few names that come up often in Tallinn's expat community:

Kalli-Kalli International Kindergarten

An international kindergarten located in Ülemiste City, a fast-growing business district home to a lot of tech company and startup employees in Tallinn. Kalli-Kalli has been operating since 2011 and hosts around 100 children across several class groups. It's also in the same area as Emili Elementary School and the International School of Tallinn, which makes for a fairly convenient transition if your child later continues at an international primary school nearby. More info at kalli.ee.

Tallinn International Kindergarten

An international kindergarten with a subsidy arrangement tied to the Tallinn Education Department, so children registered as Tallinn residents still receive part of the municipal subsidy even at this private kindergarten. There's also a discount for families with more than one child enrolled. Monthly fees depend on your child's attendance schedule, so it's best to inquire directly. Info at kindergarten.ee.

Väike Päike (Suve Kindergarten)

Väike Päike is a childcare and kindergarten network that's been around since 2003, with several locations across Tallinn, the Harju county area, and Tartu. One of its units, Suve Kindergarten in central Tallinn, runs an international group taught in English with a STEAM approach. Good fit if you're after a smaller-scale, more personal setting compared to the larger international schools. More info at lasteklubi.ee.

International School of Tallinn (IST)

One of the largest and most established international schools in Tallinn, enrolling students aged 3 to 19. Annual tuition for the 2025/2026 school year ranges from roughly 8,690 to 11,715 euros depending on grade level, not including extracurriculars and aftercare. There's a family discount of around 10-20% for a second child and beyond, plus an additional discount for parents employed at companies based in Ülemiste City. Full fee details at ist.ee/admission/tuition-fees.

International School of Estonia (ISE)

Another well-known international school, with tuition that scales up by grade level: around 5,000 euros a year for pre-school, rising to around 16,000 euros for kindergarten through grade 5, around 17,500 euros for grades 6-10, and around 18,000 euros for grades 11-12. There's also a one-time registration fee of around 250 euros for pre-school and 500 euros for K-12. ISE also offers merit- and needs-based scholarships. Full details and discount schemes at ise.edu.ee/join-us/fees-and-tuition-discounts.

Practical Tips Before You Apply

  • Start researching as early as possible. Ideally before you even relocate, especially for international schools that have limited spots and a selection process that can take a few weeks.
  • Sort out your official address first. Registering for public school or kindergarten requires a home address that's already registered in Estonia's population registry, so the order matters: official address first, then you can join the school queue.
  • Budget for more than just tuition. For international schools, ask about aftercare, extracurricular fees, uniforms, and transport too, since these often aren't included in the headline tuition number.
  • Consider a mixed path. Some families start their kids at an international school while they pick up Estonian, then switch to a public school once the child is comfortable with the language. This is fairly common, and there's no restriction on switching tracks partway through.

Sorting out your kids' schooling is one part of relocation that needs the earliest planning, sometimes even before you've wrapped up your own residence permit. But once your address and queue applications are in motion, the rest is fairly manageable since the system is reasonably transparent and the information is easy to find online.
