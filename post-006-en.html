The Estonian Relocation Paperwork: Visa, Residence Permit, to Family Doctor

August 2026 · 15 min read

After getting the offer, I thought the hardest part was over. It wasn't. There's another chapter that rarely gets covered in content about working abroad: relocation paperwork. Not about skills or interviews anymore, but about visas, residence permits, address registration, all the way to signing up with a family doctor.

I'm writing this generally rather than as one specific company's procedure, because the broad steps of relocation paperwork to Estonia for foreign workers follow roughly the same path, whether you're relocated by a large company with its own global mobility team or handling most of the process yourself. The small details can differ depending on your employer and whichever agency is helping, but the overall sequence stays consistent.

Overview: Eight Stages

Broadly, the relocation process to Estonia for an employer-sponsored worker consists of:

  1. Short-term employment registration
  2. Long-stay D-visa
  3. Traveling to Estonia
  4. Temporary residence permit (TRP) or EU Blue Card
  5. Finding a permanent home
  6. Address registration
  7. Tax registration
  8. Family doctor registration
If you're being relocated through a company, there's usually a team or agency handling the administrative side, and you mostly just fill in your data and prepare documents. Still, it's worth understanding the flow yourself, so you're not caught off guard partway through and know which documents to prepare early.

Stage 1: Short-Term Employment Registration

This is the first step, and it's usually handled by the employer electronically through the Estonian Police and Border Guard Board's online system. Short-term employment registration functions as an initial "work permit" in Estonia, and it needs to be in place before you can apply for the long-stay D-visa, since it serves as proof of your travel purpose.

The process is usually fast, a decision typically comes within 48 hours. From this registration you'll also get an Estonian identification code, an 11-digit code based on sex and date of birth that stays with you for life and is used for identification across various systems, including tax and social insurance.

Stage 2: Long-Stay D-Visa

The long-stay D-visa can be valid for up to 365 days with multiple entries. With this visa plus the short-term employment registration, you're already allowed to start working as soon as you arrive in Estonia.

A few technical things worth checking well in advance:

  • Your passport must have been issued within the last 10 years.
  • It needs at least two blank pages for the visa.
  • It must remain valid for at least 3 months past the visa's expiration date, with a minimum total validity of 1 year.
  • Prepare a passport photo sized 35x45mm, since this is usually the one thing you genuinely have to handle yourself.

D-visa applications usually need to be submitted 14-30 days before your start date, depending on the embassy's practice, since the process can legally take up to 30 days. Estonia doesn't have an embassy in every country. For applicants from Indonesia, for example, the nearest embassy is typically in Singapore, so the visa appointment likely needs to happen there rather than at home.

One thing I learned: this visa process is purely a consul's decision, so it can't be sped up no matter how anxiously you're waiting. The best way to handle it is preparing your documents as early as possible, so there's no delay coming from your side.

Stage 3: Traveling to Estonia

Usually the employer or relocation agency helps book temporary accommodation for the first few weeks, so you don't have to apartment hunt remotely before actually arriving in Estonia. Travel tickets are also typically booked for you once your departure date and airport are confirmed.

Flight class usually follows each company's own travel policy, so it can vary. What matters across every case: keep every receipt during the trip, especially for extra costs like additional luggage or taxis, because without receipts reimbursement usually can't be processed.

Stage 4: Temporary Residence Permit or EU Blue Card

This is the stage that takes the most time and causes the most confusion, so I'll go into more detail.

TRP for Employment

A Temporary Residence Permit (TRP) for employment can be issued for up to 5 years. It's tied to your sponsoring company, so if you switch employers, you'll need to apply for a new residence permit under a new sponsor. To remain in Estonia, you need a valid sponsor until you're eventually eligible to apply for permanent settlement.

After living in Estonia for 3 years and passing an A2-level Estonian language exam, you can apply for a residence permit for settling permanently. After living continuously in Estonia for at least 5 years with a valid residence permit, you can apply for a permanent residence permit. If you extend your TRP after 5 years in Estonia, you're required to pass an A2-level language exam. For a long-term residence permit, a B1 level is required.

Under recent legal changes, learning Estonian has become mandatory, with a minimum of A2 level required after 5 years of residence. Some companies offer free Estonian language courses for foreign employees as part of their relocation benefits, so it's worth asking HR about this early on.

After applying for a residence permit, the Police and Border Guard Board has up to two months to process the application. You'll get a decision letter by email, then wait for a second email confirming your residence permit card is ready for pickup, usually around 10 days later, at the same office where the appointment took place.

Once you have a residence permit, you no longer need a visa to travel in the Schengen area, and you can stay in other Schengen countries for up to 90 days within a 180-day period. When traveling, you still need to carry both your original passport and your residence permit card.

Worth keeping in mind: since a TRP is tied to a sponsor, if your employment contract ends, you'll need to find a new sponsor or leave the Schengen area. There's no strict legally defined grace period, but it's typically around 30 to 90 days.

EU Blue Card as an Alternative

For some cases, an EU Blue Card can be an alternative to a standard TRP. Under the Estonian Aliens Act, EU Blue Cards are available without a specific quota restriction for IT positions, including engineering and product management. For roles outside of that, the application falls under an annual immigration quota that's fairly limited, while demand far exceeds the quota, and it's usually filled within the first week of the year. So if your role isn't in IT, you'll most likely be directed toward the standard TRP path instead of an EU Blue Card.

Stage 5: Finding a Permanent Home

While staying in temporary accommodation, this is when you start looking for a permanent place. A few property portals commonly used in Estonia:

  • City24
  • KV.ee
  • Kinnisvara24

The rental market in Tallinn moves fast, good listings are usually only available for a few weeks. If you see something that fits, it's worth acting on it right away rather than sitting on it.

Once you've found a suitable place and are ready to sign, budget for three cost components that typically come up at the start:

  • One month's rent in advance.
  • A broker fee, usually equivalent to one month's rent.
  • A deposit, usually equal to 1-2 months' rent.

In other words, prepare enough funds for 3-4 months' worth of rent upfront. This tends to surprise people who've never rented in Europe before.

Stage 6: Address Registration

Address registration is mandatory once you have a residence permit and have lived in Estonia for one month or more. You'll need a signed rental agreement or an owner's agreement letter. Registration can be done through the e-Rahvastikuregister system.

Once your address is registered, you become eligible for free public transport in Tallinn, and you can register with the family doctor system so consultations become free as well.

Stage 7: Tax Registration

Under the Estonian Income Tax Act, you're considered a tax resident if you meet at least one of two conditions: your place of residence is in Estonia, or you stay in Estonia for at least 183 days over a 12-month period.

Because you've already registered an address, you're automatically considered a tax resident from your arrival date. Even so, you're still required to submit an R form to the Tax and Customs Board to complete your residency status in the state system.

Each year, from mid-February to the end of April, you can submit your tax declaration through the Tax and Customs Board's online system. In Estonia, your employer calculates and pays taxes on your behalf, so the salary you receive each month is already net, no additional payment is required from you.

Stage 8: Family Doctor Registration

Everyone living in Estonia should have a registered family doctor. Family doctors each cover a specific service area, so you'll need to check who's closest and speaks English.

Your family doctor handles everyday healthcare needs, from routine checkups to prescriptions. If you get sick and need sick leave, you're usually required to notify your family doctor the same day.

Once this stage is done, your relocation process is administratively complete. It feels like finally checking off the last box on a very long list.

If You're Relocating With Family

If you're moving with a spouse or children, most of the steps mirror your own: the long-stay D-visa, travel booking, then a TRP as a dependent, which can also be issued for up to 5 years. Once their residence permit comes through, you can help them with address registration, tax, and family doctor registration, same as your own process.

A few extra things to prepare specifically for family members:

  • Apostilled documents. Marriage certificates and children's birth certificates need to be apostilled and given a sworn translation into English if the original doesn't already include one. For applicants from Indonesia, the apostille process is handled by the Ministry of Law and Human Rights through the apostille.ahu.go.id portal. It's best to start this well in advance since it takes time and is needed before the visa and TRP appointments.
  • Health insurance for a spouse. A spouse usually isn't automatically covered by state health insurance right away, so independent health insurance is needed until their resident status is finalized. A basic package from a local insurance provider like Ergo is usually enough for this. If you have a child under 3 and your spouse isn't working, once your spouse gets their TRP, the child will automatically be covered by state health insurance until age 3.
  • Health insurance for children. State health insurance for children is valid until age 19, but it only kicks in after address registration is complete, which usually takes a few months after arrival. Because of that, arrange independent insurance for roughly the first 6 months to cover that transition period.

The Documents You'll Usually Be Asked For

These are the documents most commonly requested during the Estonian relocation process, whether handled through an agency or on your own:

  • Passport copies for everyone relocating.
  • A family data form, usually available on the Estonian Police and Border Guard Board's website.
  • A biographical data form, also available on the same website.
  • Digital passport-style photos, for you and any children.
  • A CV or resume, for you and your spouse.
  • Relevant certificates and work records.
  • An additional form if you've had any military service.
  • Education documents certified by a foreign qualification recognition body, or proof of work experience, if applying for an EU Blue Card.

If you're being relocated through a company, all of this is usually uploaded to whatever internal relocation platform your employer or agency uses. The specific platform varies depending on who's handling your process, so this part is best confirmed directly with your HR team or relocation agency.

On Reimbursement

Many relocation-related costs, from extra baggage fees to local transport, can usually be reimbursed by your employer according to the relocation agreement. The general rule that almost always applies: keep every receipt, and submit your expense report as soon as possible, ideally within 2 months of the expense being incurred. The longer you wait, the higher the risk the claim can't be processed.

What I Took Away From This

  • Check your passport validity early. A lot of visa problems can be avoided just by checking this well in advance.
  • Don't put off family documents. Apostilles and sworn translations take time, so if you're relocating with family, start this as early as possible.
  • Keep every receipt from day one. Relocation reimbursements can add up to a meaningful amount, don't lose it because you forgot to save a receipt.
  • Register your address as soon as you can. A lot of other things, from free public transport to a family doctor, only open up once this is done.
  • Don't take the home search lightly. The rental market in Tallinn moves fast, and your temporary accommodation window is limited.

Closing Thoughts

This process is long, but each step has a clear reason behind it and the sequence makes sense once you understand it. What actually wears you down usually isn't the complexity of any single step, but the sheer number of documents and appointments you have to coordinate within a tight window.

If you're going through something similar, I hope this gives you a clearer picture of what's ahead, so you can prepare your documents and your patience from the start, instead of getting caught off guard along the way.
