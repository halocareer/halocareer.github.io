A Mock Business Interview for Data Analysts: Case Studies, Budget, and Behavioral Questions

August 2026 · 15 min read

After the technical interview, the next stage was the business interview. The interviewer was a business lead, in my case someone leading marketing for one of their business lines. Not a single line of code in this session. It's all about how you think through ambiguous business problems, and how you tell the story of your own work experience.

Same as the previous article, this is reconstructed from my notes, not a word-for-word transcript. I've rebuilt it so the flow is clear and useful as a reference.

Part 1: The Most Impactful Project

Interviewer: "Tell me about your most impactful project, and what impact you personally brought to it."

Me: "One of the most memorable is an early demand capture project at a travel-tech company I worked at previously. I'll walk through it using STAR to keep it clear. The situation: large campaigns were often run reactively, only kicking off after demand had already started rising. This meant we were losing bookings in the early window and A&P spend wasn't efficient because everything was rushed at the last minute."

"My task at the time was to find a way to detect demand earlier and improve campaign timing, while coordinating with the supply, pricing, marketing, and finance teams."

"What I did: I analyzed early signals like search volume, page views, and booking patterns broken down by product category and destination. From that I found that flight searches typically rose three weeks earlier than the campaign peak, while hotel conversion followed about a week later. Based on that finding, I shifted campaign timing earlier, ran A/B tests on banners and storefronts, and built a budget tracker for continuous ROI monitoring, plus automated alerts for anomalies. I also coordinated constantly with campaign marketing, supply commercial, and FP&A to keep the metrics consistent across teams."

"The result: early-window bookings up 15%, reactive A&P spend down 12%, and campaign revenue 10% above target. This framework was eventually adopted for peak season and long-weekend planning going forward as well."

Interviewer: "Interesting. But I'm curious about that 12% spend reduction. Does that mean you deliberately cut spending, or was there something else behind it?"

Me: "Good question. The objective of this project was never to minimize spend. The objective was improving efficiency and timing. That 12% reduction was a side effect, not the goal. It came from three things: capturing demand earlier so there was no need for sudden burst spending, reallocating budget based on actual performance, and avoiding reactive spending in the final minutes, which tends to be less efficient. What matters is that the campaign revenue target was still met and exceeded. So this isn't a story about saving money, it's a story about lower spend with a stronger outcome, which is what I'd call an efficiency gain."

Note: this is the single most important follow-up in the whole session. If I'd just answered "because we spent less money," it would have sounded like the project was about cost-cutting, when the real point was about allocation efficiency. That framing is what separates a junior-level story from a senior-level one.

Interviewer: "How did you monitor this project's performance on an ongoing basis?"

Me: "I tracked a few layers of metrics daily: revenue, bookings, traffic, and conversion rate for baseline performance. For marketing efficiency, cost, ROI, and incremental ROI where the data was available. For campaign health, budget pacing, inventory availability, and price competitiveness. All those signals fed into weekly budget reallocation decisions."

Part 2: The Budget Allocation Case Study

Interviewer: "Now a case study. You have a marketing budget of ten million euros and can't add more. How do you allocate it across markets?"

Me: "Before I get into numbers, I'd want to clarify the objective first, whether it's revenue, market share, contribution margin, or customer acquisition. Let's assume the objective is revenue and mid-term growth."

"My steps: first, segment markets by size, maturity level, and competitive intensity. Second, look at historical performance in each market, including ROI, CAC, and contribution margin. Third, and this is the most important part, I wouldn't allocate purely based on historical ROI. I'd try to estimate marginal ROI, meaning the expected return from the next euro invested in that market. A market with a high historical ROI doesn't necessarily still have a high marginal return if it's already close to saturation."

"Fourth, I'd also weigh strategic priorities and operational constraints, for example a market that's a current expansion focus even if its historical ROI isn't as high as a more mature market. Fifth, I'd set an initial allocation with a portion set aside for testing, then monitor and reallocate periodically based on actual performance, rather than locking it in at the start of the year."

Interviewer: "If you were asked to build management-level KPIs to evaluate hundreds of millions of euros in marketing investment across many markets, what would you use?"

Me: "I'd group it into a few layers. Business outcome: revenue, transactions, contribution margin, market share. Customer: new customers, CAC, conversion, retention, LTV. Marketing efficiency: ROI, incremental ROI, cost per incremental transaction, payback period. One thing I'd always emphasize to stakeholders: ROI shows efficiency, but incremental ROI is what shows genuinely causal business impact. Both matter, and neither can fully substitute for the other."

Part 3: B2B vs. B2C

Interviewer: "Our company also has a B2B line. What do you think differentiates B2B from B2C marketing, and can the approach be the same?"

Me: "They can't be treated the same. B2C is usually high volume, individual decision-makers, shorter decision cycles, and heavily transaction-driven. B2B has lower volume, involves many stakeholders, longer decision cycles, and is more contract and long-term relationship-driven. As an analyst, the funnel I'd use also needs to differ. For B2C I'd look at reach, acquisition, conversion, transaction, retention. For B2B I'd look at lead, qualified lead, sales process, contract, usage, then retention or expansion. If I force one framework onto both, I'll end up misreading performance signals on one side or the other."

Part 4: Behavioral Questions

Interviewer: "Tell me about a professional disagreement you've had, and how you resolved it."

Me: "There was a difference of opinion around metric definitions and campaign priorities between my team and another team. We had different perspectives, not that one side was wrong. I tried to understand their point of view first, looked for objective criteria we could both agree on, then brought data to compare the trade-offs of each option. We eventually agreed on a single metric definition that got documented, so the same ambiguity wouldn't come up again in the future."

Interviewer: "Tell me about a mistake you've made."

Me: shares a genuine analytical mistake, its impact, then closes with: "After that I took full ownership, fixed the result, and more importantly, changed the process so a similar mistake wouldn't happen again. I think that's worth more than claiming I've never made a big mistake, which obviously wouldn't be realistic."

Interviewer: "Do you prefer comfort or challenge?"

Me: "Challenge. But I don't mean endless overtime. I enjoy situations where the problem is ambiguous and I have to find my own way forward, rather than just executing clear instructions."

Interviewer: "Are you someone who builds things from scratch, or more of an incremental improver?"

Me: "I'd say builder from scratch, but pragmatic. I'm comfortable building a framework when there's no foundation yet. But if a good foundation already exists, I won't rebuild it just to look innovative. I check what's already working before deciding whether to build or improve."

Interviewer: "Last one. Say the Ads team and the Lifecycle team both need your help, but you only have capacity for one. How do you decide?"

Me: "I'd first clarify the objective behind each request, estimate the impact and effort involved, look at urgency, and consider the opportunity cost of choosing one over the other. I'd also check for dependencies and how this aligns with the company's overall priorities. Then I'd make the call and communicate the reasoning transparently to both teams, including when I could help the other one. Bottom line, prioritization isn't about which stakeholder matters more, it's about which problem creates higher expected value for the company."

Note: prioritization questions like this often get answered by rushing to pick one team. What's more convincing is showing the thinking process first, then landing on a decision.

Closing Thoughts

The business interview felt much more personal than the technical one. It's not about being technically right or wrong, but about whether the way I think and tell my story can be trusted by someone who'll eventually be a colleague.

In the next article I'll wrap up with the final interview, the most relaxed stage in terms of format but in my view the most decisive.
