#002 Job Hunting

Five Interview Stages for a Data Analyst Role at a Global Ride-Hailing Company: Prep, Tips, and Use Cases

August 2026 · 16 min read

A while back I got the chance to go through the hiring process for a Senior Data Analyst, Marketing role at a ride-hailing and mobility company operating in dozens of countries. I'm deliberately not naming the company in this post. Not because it's a secret, but because the stages and questions I went through are actually pretty common in senior data analyst hiring processes at almost any tech company. So it's more useful written up as a general lesson, not a review of one specific company.

The process itself was fairly long: five stages, from the first phone call with a recruiter to the final interview with a would-be manager. Total time from first contact to a final decision was a bit over a month. In this post I'm summing up everything I learned, what to prepare for each stage, the types of use cases that typically come up for a marketing data analyst role, and how to answer them.

Overview: Five Stages

Every company has its own variation, but this general structure is fairly common for a Senior Data Analyst role at a mid-to-large tech company:

  • Recruiter's Call (20-30 minutes) — initial screening on background, motivation, and expectations.
  • Home Task (5-7 day deadline) — a data analysis and business research case study.
  • Technical Interview (1 hour) — SQL, Python, statistics, A/B testing, discussion of the home task.
  • Business Interview (1 hour) — problem solving, business acumen, behavioral questions.
  • Final Interview (1 hour) — culture fit, values, and whether the team wants to work with you.
The first lesson I took away: don't prepare for every stage the same way. The process moves step by step from "can you do the analysis?" to "can you solve a business problem?" to finally "do we actually want to work with you?"

Each stage tests something different, so preparation should be different too. A recruiter call isn't a technical interview. A final interview isn't about proving you can code anymore. If you treat every stage the same, your answers usually end up generic and miss the point.

Stage 1: Recruiter's Call

This is not a technical interview. The recruiter wants to know whether your background fits the role, why you want to move, why you're interested in this particular company, and whether your expectations on salary and role are aligned.

What to prepare

Prepare a 60-90 second pitch with the structure Current → Previous → Expertise → Motivation. Don't narrate your entire CV. Focus on your current role, a brief career progression, 3-4 skills most relevant to the role you're applying for, then connect it to why you're interested in the company.

For "why this company", avoid a generic answer like "because it's a big global company." The recruiter has heard that answer hundreds of times. It's stronger if you tie it to the scale and complexity of what they do, then connect that to your specific experience. For example, if the company operates in many countries, you can say the multi-market scale is what appeals to you because you want to apply your marketing analytics experience in a much broader environment.

If you've held a managerial position and are now applying for a purely individual contributor (IC) role, prepare an answer for that too. I got asked this because the role I applied for was purely IC, while at the time I was still leading a small team. The answer I used went roughly like this: even though I lead a team, I've stayed very hands-on with data and enjoy solving problems directly, so the IC role is actually a chance to go deeper into analytics, while the experience of leading a team is still useful for mentoring and cross-team collaboration.

Stage 2: Home Task

The home task usually comes with a deadline of around 5-7 days and consists of two parts: data analysis and business research. The point isn't just to see whether you can process data, but whether you understand the business context behind it.

The structure I used for my answer

For the data analysis part, don't open a notebook and start coding right away. Follow this flow instead:

  1. Business question — what business decision is this analysis supposed to support?
  2. Metric — which metric is relevant, for example revenue, bookings, CVR, ROI, CAC?
  3. Check the data — check the grain, missing values, duplicates, outliers, date range, and definitions of each column.
  4. Analysis — only then move into trends, segmentation, comparisons, correlation, or experiments.
  5. Recommendation — don't stop at the insight. Always close with "therefore, I would recommend..."

For the business research part, don't just collect facts. Use the framework Market → Customer → Competition → Economics → Opportunity, then always close with the question "what does this mean for the company?"

How to present the home task

When asked to walk through the solution, I learned not to overwhelm the interviewer with every piece of analysis I'd done. The presentation structure I used: start with the business problem, briefly explain the methodology, show one or two of the most important findings, explain the business implication, give a recommendation, then close with limitations.

Mentioning limitations matters, especially at a senior level. For example: "one limitation here is that this is observational data, so I'd be careful about interpreting this relationship as causal."

Stage 3: Technical Interview

This is the most technical stage, usually with a Lead Data Analyst from the relevant team. What's being tested isn't just syntax, it's analytical thinking. Here are a few types of use cases I ran into and how I answered them.

Use case: calculating ROI from two tables with different grains

I was given two tables. The first had daily cost by channel and city. The second had revenue per order, with channel and city columns as well. The question: calculate ROI (revenue divided by cost) by date and channel.

The trap is in the grain. The revenue table sits at the order level, while the cost table sits at the date-channel-city level. If you JOIN them directly without aggregating first, revenue gets duplicated because one cost row can match many order rows. The correct approach: aggregate each table down to the same grain first (date, channel, city), then JOIN.

The biggest lesson from this technical session: always ask "what's the grain of each table?" before writing a single line of query. Grain mistakes are one of the most common but most expensive bugs in analytics.

Use case: cost is zero or NULL

Follow-up to the ROI case above: what if there's revenue but cost is zero or NULL? The wrong answer is jumping straight to "just use COALESCE." The right approach is understanding the business meaning first. Zero cost could mean organic traffic or genuinely no spend. NULL cost usually means missing data or a pipeline issue. Those two conditions mean different things, so they need to be handled differently. For ROI, if cost is zero, the result should be NULL or undefined, not infinity.

Use case: top 2 ROI per channel

Follow-up question: get the two highest-ROI days for each channel. This tests window functions. The flow: calculate daily ROI, PARTITION BY channel, ORDER BY ROI descending, then take rank less than or equal to two. You can use a CTE with ROW_NUMBER, RANK, or DENSE_RANK depending on whether tied ROI values should count as one rank or not.

Use case: simple Python coding, but testing your understanding

I was asked to write a function that returns all divisors of a number. The solution is simple, loop from 1 to n and check the modulo. What mattered more was the follow-up: I was asked to explain the code line by line, then asked whether it could be optimized. The answer: you only need to loop up to the square root of n, because divisors always come in pairs.

This is an important lesson: the interviewer isn't just checking whether your code runs. They're checking whether you understand what you wrote and can explain the trade-offs.

Use case: designing an A/B test

This was the part that got dug into the most. The case involved a registration funnel (in a ride-hailing company's context, this would be a driver registration funnel) with heavy drop-off midway through. The proposal: if a user has no activity for two hours, send an automated call as a reminder. I was asked to design the experiment.

The structure I used:

  • Hypothesis — the reminder call increases funnel completion rate.
  • Population — users who reached a certain step but hadn't progressed after two hours.
  • Randomization — random split between treatment (receives the call) and control (regular communication or none).
  • Primary metric — incremental completion rate, not just call pickup rate or CTR.
  • Secondary metrics — conversion between steps, time to completion.
  • Guardrails — complaints, opt-outs, cost, and negative user experience.
A point that's often missed: the primary metric has to align with the business objective, not whatever's easiest to measure. Call pickup rate is easy to look at, but what actually matters is whether it leads to registration.

Use case: sample size larger than the available population

A fairly tricky follow-up: what if user volume is low, and the MDE (minimum detectable effect) you want requires a sample size larger than the available population? Don't panic and don't just say "run it longer", because if the population is genuinely small, more time might not solve the problem.

A better approach: first validate whether that MDE is actually realistic and business-meaningful, maybe you need to widen it. Consider pooling comparable markets to get a larger population, or use sequential testing or a Bayesian approach that can give a decision earlier with limited data. If it turns out statistically impossible to reach adequate power, communicate that limitation explicitly to stakeholders, rather than pretending the result is conclusive.

Use case: experiment economics and the rollout decision

If an experiment shows an uplift, don't stop at "it's statistically significant." Calculate the economics too: incremental conversions, cost per incremental conversion, the economic value of each one, then incremental ROI, before finally getting to a rollout decision.

Use case: attribution vs. causal inference

A classic question: "how do you know the booking came from the campaign?" Here it's important to distinguish attribution (who gets the credit) from causal inference (what would have happened without the campaign). The counterfactual is the outcome that would have happened to treated users if they hadn't received the campaign. The most solid way to get a counterfactual is a randomized experiment with a holdout group. If that's not feasible, alternatives include geo experiments, matched controls, difference-in-differences, or synthetic control.

I was also asked whether causal inference is built into the pipeline or done ad hoc. My answer: it depends on the scale of the campaign. For routine, lower-budget campaigns, standardized attribution and monitoring are enough, not every campaign needs sophisticated causal inference. But for strategic, high-budget campaigns, I'd go deeper, using a holdout group and causal analysis, because the decisions made from that carry much bigger stakes.

Stage 4: Business Interview

The interviewer at this stage is usually a business lead from the relevant team, in my case someone leading marketing for one of their business lines. The focus is no longer queries or code, but how you think through ambiguous business problems.

STAR story for "most impactful project"

I used a story about an early demand capture project I worked on at a travel-tech company I worked at previously. To keep the answer tight and easy to follow, I always lay it out in a clear STAR order rather than letting it drift as a loose narrative:

  • Situation — large campaigns were often run reactively after demand had already started climbing, causing lost bookings in the early window and inefficient A&P spend.
  • Task — I was tasked with finding a way to detect demand earlier and improve campaign timing, while coordinating across the supply, pricing, marketing, and finance teams.
  • Action — I analyzed early demand signals like search volume and page views, found that flight searches typically rose three weeks earlier than the campaign peak, then shifted campaign timing earlier, ran A/B tests on banners and storefronts, and built a budget tracker to monitor ROI continuously.
  • Result — early-window bookings up 15%, reactive A&P spend down 12%, and campaign revenue 10% above target. The framework was later adopted for peak-season planning too.

That's the order I stick to for every story-based question: don't jump straight to Action before Situation and Task are clear, and don't stop before naming a measurable Result.

The challenging follow-up: "why did spend drop 12%?"

This follow-up was fairly critical. The wrong answer: "because we spent less money." The correct framing: the objective was never to minimize spend, it was to improve efficiency. The 12% reduction came from capturing demand earlier, reallocating budget based on performance, and avoiding reactive spending in the final minutes, while the campaign target was still met and exceeded. Lower spend plus a stronger outcome means efficiency improved, not just savings.

Use case: allocating a marketing budget across markets

The case: "you have a budget of several million euros, how do you allocate it across different markets?" The framework I used:

  1. Clarify the objective — revenue, market share, or customer acquisition?
  2. Segment markets by size, maturity, and competitive intensity.
  3. Look at historical performance in each market.
  4. Estimate marginal ROI, not just historical ROI.
  5. Consider strategic priorities and operational constraints.
  6. Set an initial allocation, monitor, then reallocate periodically.
The most important point here: don't allocate purely based on historical ROI. The better question is "what's the expected return from the next euro invested in this market?" That's what's called marginal ROI, and it's what separates a junior answer from a senior one.

Use case: management-level KPIs

Asked what KPIs I'd use to evaluate marketing investment at a management level across many markets. I grouped it into three: business outcome (revenue, transactions, contribution margin), customer (new customers, CAC, retention, LTV), and marketing efficiency (ROI, incremental ROI, cost per incremental transaction, payback period). Key point: ROI shows efficiency, while incremental ROI shows genuinely causal business impact.

Use case: B2B vs. B2C

Since the company also had a B2B line, I was asked about the difference in B2B versus B2C marketing approaches. In short, B2C has high volume, individual decision-makers, and shorter decision cycles. B2B has lower volume, many stakeholders, longer cycles, and is more contract and relationship-driven. As an analyst, the funnel and the KPIs used also need to differ, you can't use the same framework for both.

Behavioral questions that came up

A few behavioral questions I ran into and how I approached them:

  • Professional disagreement — frame it as "we had different perspectives", not "they were wrong." Close with how you found objective evidence to reach alignment.
  • Mistake — don't say "I've never made a major mistake." The strongest answer actually owns a mistake, explains the impact, takes ownership, and describes the process improvement that followed.
  • Comfort vs. challenge — pick challenge, but clarify what you mean. Not endless overtime, but enjoying ambiguous problems where you have to find the path forward yourself.
  • Prioritization — when two teams both need help but you only have capacity for one, the key is explaining that prioritization isn't about which stakeholder matters more, but which problem creates the highest expected value for the company.

Stage 5: Final Interview

In the final stage, the interviewer is usually a manager from the team you'd be joining. Technical ability is assumed proven by this point. The focus shifts to: is this person good to work with?

The dimensions usually assessed: ownership, communication skills, cross-functional collaboration, the ability to say "no" or prioritize, autonomy, and growth mindset.

Prepare at least 6-8 STAR stories

I learned one story isn't enough. Prepare stories for: your biggest achievement, a difficult stakeholder, a professional disagreement, a failure or mistake, prioritizing between two important requests, building something from scratch, an experience leading a team, and a complex technical challenge.

One detail I learned: memorize the logic, not the exact wording. If you memorize it word for word, your answer sounds stiff the moment a follow-up deviates even slightly from what you rehearsed.

For achievement stories, emphasize your individual contribution

If the story is a team project, still explain your specific role. Use "my role was..." instead of repeatedly saying "we did...". The interviewer wants to know what you actually did, not just the team's overall accomplishment.

Follow-Up Questions That Are Easy to Miss

One of the biggest lessons from this whole process: the interviewer won't stop at your first answer. Here's an example exchange I went through while discussing a project's results:

Me: "ROI went up."
Interviewer: "Went up because of what?"
Me: "Budget reallocation."
Interviewer: "How did you know which budget to move?"
Me: "Continuous performance monitoring."
Interviewer: "Which metrics specifically?"
Me: "ROI, revenue, conversion rate..."
Interviewer: "But how do you know the campaign caused the revenue increase, not something else?"
Me: "That's where incrementality becomes important..."

So don't just prepare an answer. Prepare the next three levels of "why" that might follow it.

A Universal Framework for Answering Case Questions

For both technical and business open-ended questions, I consistently used this framework:

  1. Clarify — "before jumping into a solution, I'd like to clarify the objective."
  2. Define success — "I would define success as..."
  3. Break down — "I'd look at this across a few areas..."
  4. Analyze — "first, I would..."
  5. Validate — "I'd validate whether this relationship is actually causal..."
  6. Recommend — "based on that, I would recommend..."
  7. Measure — "finally, I would monitor..."

The same framework applies to behavioral questions too, just shaped as Situation, Task, Action, Result, closed with what you learned.

Five Biggest Lessons From the Whole Process

  • Don't jump straight to a solution. Clarify the objective before diving into anything technical.
  • Always think about the grain of the data, especially before writing any SQL query.
  • Don't confuse correlation with causation. Always ask "what would have happened without this campaign?"
  • Don't optimize a single metric blindly. Revenue can go up while profit goes down. Always consider guardrails.
  • If you don't know, don't bluff. Say "I haven't worked directly with that method, but my understanding is..., and here's how I'd approach the problem..." This actually shows analytical maturity, not weakness.

Closing Thoughts

This process taught me that interviewing for a senior data analyst role isn't just about whether you've memorized analytical techniques. What's being tested is whether you can carry a chain of thinking from the business problem, to the analytical question, to the data, to the insight, to statistical and causal confidence, all the way to a business decision and a measurable impact.

If you're preparing for a similar interview, my advice: don't just practice SQL and statistics. Practice telling your experience with a clear structure, and practice thinking out loud in front of someone else without jumping straight to the answer. That's what most often separates candidates who pass from those who don't.
