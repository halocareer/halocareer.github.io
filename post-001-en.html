#001 Job Hunting

How I Applied for a Job in Estonia With No Connections at All

July 2026 · 7 min read

When I first got serious about working abroad, Estonia wasn't the first country that came to mind. I didn't know anyone there, had no idea how the job market worked, and honestly, I barely knew what kind of country Estonia even was.

What I did know: I'd been working as a data analyst in Jakarta for two years, felt like it was time to try something different, and believed the skills I had should be portable pretty much anywhere.

Turns out believing that wasn't enough on its own. But it also turns out you don't need connections if you know how to go about it.

Why Estonia?

Estonia ended up on my radar for a fairly pragmatic reason: a lot of tech companies there hire internationally, and the work permit process is relatively more straightforward compared to other Western European countries.

I started researching through expat forums, articles about digital nomads in Eastern Europe, and Reddit threads about working in the Baltic states. Estonia kept coming up with a consistent reputation: a solid startup ecosystem, reasonable taxes, and a government that's genuinely tech-savvy.

One thing that convinced me: Estonia is the only country in the world with "e-Residency", a digital identity program for non-citizens. That's a strong signal they're serious about being open to outsiders.

After about two weeks of research, I decided Estonia would be my main target. Not the only one, but the main one.

Starting the Application Process

I started with the obvious move: LinkedIn. But not just clicking "Easy Apply" everywhere. There were a few things I did first before applying anywhere.

First, I updated my LinkedIn profile properly. Professional photo, a clear headline ("Data Analyst | SQL, Python, Looker | Open to Relocation"), and a summary written in English with a conversational but still professional tone. Not a copy-pasted template.

Second, I set my search location to Tallinn and other major Estonian cities. Filters: Data Analyst, Data Engineer, Business Intelligence, anything relevant to my background.

Third, I turned on "Open to Work" but set it visible only to recruiters, not to everyone.

Platforms I Used

LinkedIn was clearly the main one, but I also used a few other platforms:

  • CV.ee — the biggest job board in Estonia. A lot of local postings never show up on LinkedIn.
  • MeetFrank — good for Estonian startups, lets you chat directly with hiring managers.
  • Glassdoor — for researching salary ranges and company culture.
  • Startup Estonia job board — listings specific to Estonia's startup ecosystem.

In total, I sent around 40 applications in the first two months. That got me about 8 responses, 5 that reached the interview stage, and eventually 1 offer that I accepted.

The Cold Application That Actually Worked

One thing I didn't expect: one of the best responses I got wasn't from an open job posting at all, but from a cold application.

I researched companies in Tallinn whose products I used or was interested in. Found the Head of Data or CTO on LinkedIn. Sent a message that was short, clear, and specific, not a template, but actually written for that person and that company.

The message went something like this:

"Hi [Name], I've been using [their product] for the past year and genuinely impressed by how you handle [specific feature]. I'm a data analyst based in Jakarta with experience in [relevant skill] and I'm actively looking to relocate to Tallinn. I noticed you don't have an open DA role right now, but I'd love to share my portfolio if you think it could be useful for your team in the future."

Out of 12 cold messages I sent, I got 3 replies. One of them led to an interview and continued from there. A small conversion rate in raw numbers, but the quality of those conversations was far better than applying to a regular job posting.

What Took the Most Time

It wasn't the applying itself. What took the most time was waiting.

The normal cycle from sending an application to getting a decision could be 3-6 weeks. Some faster, some slower. I learned not to rely too heavily on any single process, and to always keep several pipelines running at once.

If you're only waiting on one company, your morale runs out before the process even finishes.

One Thing I Wish I'd Known Earlier

Timezone wasn't as big an obstacle as I expected. Video interviews could be scheduled to work for both sides, and nearly every company I applied to was already used to hiring across timezones.

What mattered more than timezone was response speed. When a recruiter emails, reply within hours, not days. That's often what separates candidates who move forward from those who don't.

Closing Thoughts

This process wasn't fast, and it wasn't easy. But it's doable.

I started from zero, with no connections in Estonia, no education background from abroad, and no prior experience working at a foreign company. What I did have was a portfolio I took seriously, a willingness to learn a different way of applying than what I was used to, and enough patience to go through the whole process.

In the next article I'll talk about the interview process itself, which turned out to be quite different from job interviews in Indonesia.
